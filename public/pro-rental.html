Pro Rental Order Processing System

Course project · ICT2112 Software Design, SIT · 2026

Role
Owner of the pricing & deposit component
Team
6 developers (one module of a three-module system)
Stack
C#, ASP.NET Core, Entity Framework Core, PostgreSQL
Patterns
Layered architecture, Facade, Adapter, dependency injection

The problem

Pro Rental, a camera-equipment rental business, handled orders manually, so customers could not see what a rental would cost until staff worked it out. Our team built the online order-processing module: catalogue, cart, checkout, payment and order tracking, integrated with separate inventory and shipping modules built by other teams.

What I built

I owned the cost-calculation component. It is the single place where every price in an order is worked out: rental cost, security deposit, delivery fee and final total. Checkout and the cart never calculate prices themselves; they call my component through its ICostCalculation interface. Delivery fees come from the shipping module through its IShippingOptionService interface, so pricing does not depend on how shipping works internally. While the shipping team's implementation was unfinished, a stub service stood in behind the same interface, so integration did not block development.

A worked example

Item Rate Days Subtotal
Canon RF 70–200mm f/2.8 L $110.00/day 13 $1,430.00
Rode VideoMic Pro+ $20.00/day 13 $260.00
Rental cost $1,690.00
Deposit (10% of rental cost) $169.00
Delivery (Same Day) $35.00
Total $1,894.00

Rental period: 19–31 March 2026 (13 days, inclusive).

Key decision: what the deposit is based on

The original requirement set the deposit at 10% of each item's value, with a minimum of S$10. Applied literally, renting a $5,000 camera for a single day would require a $500 deposit, far out of proportion to a one-day rental. I based the deposit on 10% of the rental cost instead, kept the S$10 minimum, and documented the deviation and its reasoning in the design report so the change was traceable rather than silent.

public decimal CalculateDepositAmount(decimal rentalCost)
{
    decimal deposit = rentalCost * 0.1m;
    return deposit > 10 ? deposit : 10m;
}

What I would do next

The component has no automated tests yet. Reviewing it again, these are the cases I would test first:

  • Deposit boundary: rental costs of 0, 99.99, 100 and 100.01, where the S$10 minimum gives way to the 10% rule.
  • Rounding: a deposit of 10.001 is never rounded to cents.
  • Empty order: no items still produces a S$10 deposit.
  • Invalid rental period: zero or negative days are not rejected.
  • Unknown delivery option: the fee silently becomes 0 instead of raising an error.

What I learned

Putting all pricing behind one interface made the rest of the team's work simpler: checkout and cart could change freely without touching any pricing rules. It also taught me that a requirement can be technically clear and still commercially wrong, and that changing it is fine as long as the change is documented and justified.
