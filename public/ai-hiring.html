Cross-Cultural Bias in AI Hiring

Integrated Team Project (ICT2114) with the Singapore University of Social Sciences · SIT · May–December 2026 · ongoing

Role
Dictionary designer (experimental materials)
Team
Software engineering student team, with an industry supervisor from SUSS
My part
The keyword dictionaries for job descriptions and cover letters
Tools
Excel, Python, public cover letter dataset, real job postings

The question

Large language models are increasingly used to screen job candidates and are often assumed to be objective. Much of their training data, though, is Western-centric, and Western ideals of leadership tend to reward agentic competence: being assertive, independent and individually driven. Many Asian and collectivist cultures value relational competence just as highly: collaboration, coordination and keeping a team working together. This project asks whether LLMs treat these as equally valid ways to be competent, or systematically favour one.

How the study works

The only thing that changes between versions of a candidate is how their competence is framed. That framing comes from the keyword dictionaries, which is why they had to be right before any data was generated.

My part: the dictionaries

I built two separate dictionaries, because they do different jobs: job descriptions use requirement language ("you will lead…"), while cover letters use self-presentation ("I led…").

Job description dictionary

I narrowed the supervisor's original competence dictionary down to one natural, job-description-ready form per word family, kept its sub-categories, and wrote the neutral list from scratch, checking it against the other two lists to remove overlap. After feedback, I added a dual-competence category for words and phrases that combine both styles, such as "supportive leadership".

Cover letter dictionary

I extracted agentic and communal words by comparing word frequencies between paired agentic and communal versions of the same cover letters in a public dataset. Neutral words came from vocabulary both versions share, checked against real cover letter guides for sales, supply chain and project manager roles.

Same fact, different framing

The dictionary also includes a skill-framing table: one neutral fact, written with an agentic or a communal verb. This is how two candidates can have identical experience but present it differently.

Skill Neutral Agentic framing Communal framing
Leadership Led a team Directed, spearheaded, drove Guided, mentored, supported
Teamwork Worked with others Coordinated, orchestrated Collaborated, partnered
Problem-solving Solved a problem Resolved, tackled, overcame Worked through, addressed together
Communication Communicated information Presented, persuaded, pitched Facilitated, explained, shared
Project management Managed a project Drove, executed, delivered Coordinated, supported, helped deliver
Data and analysis Analysed data Identified, uncovered, optimised Interpreted for the team, translated for stakeholders

The hardest part: competence style vs candidate quality

The most important lesson came from supervisor feedback. Some words do not signal how a candidate frames competence; they signal how good the candidate is. If those words appear in only one condition, the LLM might score that candidate higher for being better, not for being more agentic, and the experiment would measure the wrong thing.

Removed Why
strong, proven, exceptional, track record, significant impact Signal candidate quality, not competence style
honored, daily duties Make a communal candidate sound passive or overly humble
domination, crushing, conquer, unrivaled Too extreme to appear in a real cover letter
obey, subordinate, hesitant, passive Signal weakness rather than relational competence

Excluded words were kept in a separate list with a reason for each, so every removal can be explained.

Traceability

When asked why particular words such as "principled" and "qualitative" were included, I traced each one back to the wildcard expansion in the supervisor's original source file. Every word in both dictionaries can be explained by where it came from and why it stayed.

Where it fits in the study

The dictionaries generate the experimental materials. From there, the team's pipeline collected and analysed 57,600 LLM evaluations across 384 combinations of condition and region, with 7 scores per combination and 2,688 statistical tests.

What I learned

  • Much of a study's validity is decided before any data exists. A keyword list looks like a mechanical extraction task, but a few wrong words would have quietly biased every result.
  • The difference between "how someone presents" and "how good someone is" is easy to miss and essential to control. It's the same thinking as keeping a test from passing for the wrong reason.
  • Document decisions as you make them. Being asked to justify individual words weeks later showed me that reproducibility depends on the trail, not on memory.
  • Keeping the job description and cover letter dictionaries separate was less convenient, but it was the right call: they serve different linguistic purposes.
